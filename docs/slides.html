The Other Game-Changers

2:05 to 2:45 · four systems that aren’t LLMs

AlphaGo

Setting the stage before the LLM era.

  • 1997. Deep Blue beats Kasparov at chess. Go is still considered out of reach.
  • 2016. AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol 4-1. The hushed-silence moment.
  • Move 37 (Game 2). A non-human-looking move. Pros called it a mistake. It became pivotal.
  • The recipe. Huge corpus of human games + Monte Carlo Tree Search + a policy net (which moves to try) + a value net (who’s winning).
  • 2017. AlphaZero: no human games at all. Plays itself. Generalizes to chess and shogi. A clean synthetic-data story in a perfect-rule world.
  • Search + learned heuristic is now in modern LLM “thinking” modes.

full story & further reading below ↓

The full story

↑ AlphaGo (top)

Game AI still felt like search plus hand-built evaluation. Chess had been beaten by Deep Blue in 1997. But Go was different: simpler rules, a much larger search space, and a lot more shape / influence / intuition. You couldn’t make a computer play Go - at least not well.

Then, before the GPT-2 / 2.5 / 3 craze, the stage was set by AlphaGo. The room went quiet: “Oh… wow. If computers can do that… does that mean..?”

How it works. AlphaGo started with a huge corpus of human expert games. It learned a policy network for which moves look promising and a value network for who is likely winning. Monte Carlo Tree Search used those learned hunches to explore future lines.

MCTS samples promising possible futures without enumerating the whole tree. Like Spock evaluating ten steps ahead - with constraints. It decides which branches to expand, which ones to ignore, and how to back up value estimates through the search tree.

Move 37 (Game 2 vs. Lee Sedol). Lee had to go have a cigarette. Commentators immediately called it a mistake. Top pros had rarely seen anything like it - they later estimated the probability of a strong player choosing it at 1 in 10,000. It became the pivotal move that helped AlphaGo win the game. The room went quiet again. It’s iconic now: the moment people realized something genuinely new was happening.

Then AlphaGo Zero and AlphaZero (2017) threw out the human games entirely. Just played itself. Also generalized to chess and shogi. This is one of the clearest examples of synthetic data working under exact rules and exact rewards: the system generates its own training material and ends up stronger than any version trained on human play.

AlphaFold

A 50-year grand challenge in biology, cracked in one decade.

  • 1972. Anfinsen Nobel Chemistry: the amino-acid sequence determines the folded structure.
  • Levinthal’s paradox (1969). A small protein has astronomical possible shapes - yet folds in milliseconds. There must be structure to the problem.
  • Rosetta era: David Baker’s Rosetta leads early CASP work; Rosetta@home borrows idle home computers; Foldit turns folding into a game.
  • CASP13 (2018): AlphaFold jumps past the plateau, roughly 70 on the CASP scale - impressive, but below the 90-ish “solved” threshold.
  • CASP14 (2020): AlphaFold 2 clears that threshold on many targets. The core benchmark is effectively cracked.
  • AlphaFold DB: launched in 2021, expanded to ~200M structures in 2022.
  • 2024 Nobel Chemistry: Baker for protein design; Hassabis & Jumper for structure prediction.
  • 2024: AlphaFold 3 extends to protein-protein, protein-ligand, RNA, DNA interactions.
  • Architecture: Evoformer (sequence + pair representations) + geometry-aware Structure Module - turn the molecule, the prediction turns with it.

full story & further reading below ↓

The full story

↑ AlphaFold (top)

Proteins are the workhorses of biology. Channels, enzymes, antibodies, motors - your hemoglobin, your insulin, the spike on a virus. What they do depends entirely on what shape they fold into. Shape is function.

Christian Anfinsen got the 1972 Nobel for showing that the amino-acid sequence alone determines that shape. So in principle, if you know the sequence (which you can read off DNA), you should be able to predict the structure. In practice, it was the grand challenge of biology for 50+ years.

Levinthal’s paradox (1969) crystallized why it’s hard: even a small protein has astronomically many possible folds. If a protein tried every option randomly, the universe wouldn’t be old enough to finish. But real proteins fold in milliseconds. So there must be deep structure to the problem - we just couldn’t see it.

Before AlphaFold, the front-runner story was David Baker’s Rosetta. It searched for low-energy folds with hand-built physical and statistical rules. Baker’s group scaled that search with Rosetta@home which was a distributed compute system. Then Foldit put humans in the loop as players, turning protein folding into a game where people could sometimes see moves the search missed.

The biology community runs CASP every two years. Rosetta and other methods had pushed the field forward. DeepMind showed up at CASP13 in 2018 with AlphaFold 1, won clearly, and landed around 70 on the CASP scale. At CASP14 in 2020, AlphaFold 2 returned. The community basically declared the core benchmark cracked.

DeepMind released the code and the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database in 2021, then expanded the database to roughly 200 million predicted structures in 2022.

The architecture is a clean example of stacked paradigms. The Evoformer reasons jointly about the amino-acid sequence and a pairwise “which positions are talking to each other” representation, fed by a search through evolutionarily-related proteins. The Structure Module is geometry-aware, meaning if you turn the molecule, the prediction turns the same way. That geometric bias is one reason it generalizes better.

Stable Diffusion

Generation as denoising, not prediction. Math from physics, not language.

  • 2014. Goodfellow’s GANs. Worked, but unstable. Mode collapse, hard to train.
  • 2015. Sohl-Dickstein et al. - first diffusion paper. Mostly ignored.
  • 2020. DDPM (Ho et al.) makes it actually work.
  • 2022. Explosion year: DALL-E 2 (Apr), Imagen (May), Midjourney (Jul beta), Stable Diffusion (Aug, public weights/code).
  • Latent diffusion trick: do the math in a compressed latent space. Runs on a consumer GPU.
  • Math is physics: Langevin dynamics, score matching, SDEs.
  • Generalized fast: video (Sora, Veo), audio, 3D, proteins (RFdiffusion).

full story & further reading below ↓

The full story

↑ Stable Diffusion (top)

Before diffusion, the generative AI conversation was about GANs - Generative Adversarial Networks, Ian Goodfellow’s 2014 idea. Two networks, one generating, one critiquing. They worked - but they were notoriously unstable. Mode collapse. Hard to train. The images often had that “uncanny GAN look.”

Diffusion came from a completely different place: non-equilibrium statistical physics. Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015) wrote the first paper. It was largely ignored for five years. Then Ho et al. (2020) wrote the DDPM paper - Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models - and Yang Song & Stefano Ermon worked out the score-based perspective. Suddenly the math snapped together and the results started competing with, then beating, GANs.

2022 was the explosion year. DALL-E 2 in April. Imagen in May. Midjourney beta in July. Stable Diffusion in August, publicly released with weights/code. Image generation went from research toy to consumer app in months.

How it works. Take a real image and add noise until it becomes static. Train a network on randomly noised examples so it learns the denoising direction at each noise level. At generation time, start from pure static and run the trained network many times, each step nudging it toward something recognizable. Text conditioning tells it what to denoise toward.

Stable Diffusion’s specific trick is latent diffusion. Instead of doing all of that in raw pixel space - which is huge and expensive - you compress the image down to a small latent space with a separately-trained autoencoder, do the diffusion math there, then decode back to pixels at the end. That single move dropped the compute requirements enough to put image generation on a consumer GPU.

The math is physics. Langevin dynamics, score matching, stochastic differential equations. Generation is denoising, not prediction. That’s a fundamentally different paradigm from “predict the next token.” It’s part of why diffusion generalized fast: video (Sora, Veo), audio, 3D shapes, and even protein backbones (RFdiffusion applies the same denoising idea there).

This is also closely connected to energy-based models: it learns local score-like directions in the data landscape. That’s the bridge into the next section.

AlphaProof / AlphaGeometry

Math is the one place LLMs can’t bluff. Either it’s proven or it isn’t.

  • Jan 2024: AlphaGeometry (Nature). Olympiad geometry, no human-written proof demos in training.
  • Jul 2024: AlphaProof announced. Algebra & number theory via the Lean theorem prover.
  • IMO 2024: combined system scores 28/42. Silver medal. (Gold threshold: 29.)
  • Solved problems 1, 2, 4 in full. Partial on 6. Missed 3, 5 - both combinatorics.
  • Architecture: language-model/search system proposes candidates → formal verifier accepts or rejects → the proposer learns from verdicts.
  • Training: AlphaGeometry uses ~100M synthetic geometry problems; AlphaProof uses formalized proof attempts in Lean.
  • Some problems took days of search to crack.

full story & further reading below ↓

The full story

↑ AlphaProof / AlphaGeometry (top)

LLMs hallucinate. They will confidently invent citations, dates, equations. For most use cases this is annoying but manageable. Math is the one place hallucination is fatal - either the proof goes through or it doesn’t. There’s no “I’ll just sound right.”

Symbolic AI has been chasing theorem proving for decades. Mathematicians have been writing formal proofs in languages like Lean, Coq, and Isabelle for years - every step machine-checked. But it’s painfully tedious. Human in the loop everywhere.

January 2024: DeepMind publishes AlphaGeometry (Nature). For International Math Olympiad geometry problems. Architecture: a language model proposes auxiliary constructions (“draw a line here, this midpoint, that perpendicular”), and a custom symbolic geometry deduction engine chases the consequences of those constructions through the geometric axioms. Trained on ~100 million synthetically generated geometry problems - no human proof demonstrations needed.

July 2024: AlphaProof is announced. For algebra and number theory. Architecture: a language-model/search system proposes candidates and proof steps; Lean verifies them. The training story is synthetic too, but different: informal problems are translated into formal statements and the system learns from formal proof attempts.

IMO 2024: DeepMind tests both on the actual problems. Result: 28 out of 42 points, silver medal level. The gold threshold was 29 - they missed it by one point. They solved problems 1, 2, and 4 in full, got partial credit on 6, and missed problems 3 and 5 - both combinatorics, which is where the symbolic engine struggles because the search space isn’t well-structured. Some problems took days of compute to crack.

The pattern: pattern-matching alone has a ceiling (LLMs hallucinate), pure logic alone has a ceiling (combinatorial explosion, no good heuristics), but neural + symbolic sidesteps both. The neural side proposes creatively. The symbolic side checks ruthlessly. Neither’s weakness is the bottleneck.

This is the concrete neurosymbolic flagship - we’ll come back to the paradigm in the next section.

Paradigms Beyond LLMs

3:00 to 3:45 · three families plus honorable mentions

Evolutionary methods

No gradients. Just population + selection pressure.

  • 1960s-70s: Rechenberg & Schwefel (Germany) - Evolution Strategies on aerodynamic optimization.
  • 1975: Holland formalizes Genetic Algorithms.
  • 1989: Koza - Genetic Programming. The genome is an executable program.
  • 1996: CMA-ES (Hansen). Gold standard for continuous black-box optimization.
  • 2002: NEAT (Stanley & Miikkulainen) - evolves network topology, not just weights.
  • 2006: NASA’s evolved antenna flies on the ST5 mission. Outperformed conventional designs for that mission.
  • 2017: OpenAI ES competitive with deep RL on Atari. The eyebrow-raiser.
  • Killer features: no gradients, embarrassingly parallel, works on discrete / non-differentiable / black-box problems.

This is the substrate for the deep dive at 3:50.

full story & further reading below ↓

The full story

↑ Evolutionary methods (top)

Before backprop and gradient descent ate everything, evolutionary computation was a serious alternative for hard search problems. The inspiration is biological evolution: keep a population of candidate solutions, score each one with a fitness function, breed the survivors via selection + mutation + recombination, repeat.

The history is older than most people realize. Rechenberg and Schwefel developed Evolution Strategies in 1960s-70s Germany and used them for physical engineering - optimizing the shapes of aerodynamic nozzles. Holland formalized Genetic Algorithms in 1975. Koza wrote Genetic Programming in 1989 - where the genome is an executable expression tree, so you’re literally evolving programs. CMA-ES (Hansen, 1996) is still the gold standard for continuous black-box optimization - it adapts the shape of the mutation distribution to the local fitness landscape. NEAT (Stanley & Miikkulainen, 2002) evolves the topology of a neural network, not just its weights, growing complexity gradually.

The “wait, really?” moment for a lot of people was OpenAI’s 2017 ES paper: showed that a simple Evolution Strategy was competitive with deep reinforcement learning on Atari, partly because it parallelizes across thousands of workers trivially. ES doesn’t need gradients - it just needs to score policies.

Real-world: NASA’s ST5 satellite mission (2006) flew with antennas whose shape was evolved by a genetic algorithm. They look like bent paper clips. Under the mission constraints, they outperformed the conventional hand-designed options.

Why it matters: gradient descent needs a differentiable, smooth objective. Most interesting real-world objectives aren’t - they’re discrete, sparse, black-box, non-differentiable. Evolution doesn’t care. You give it a scoring function and a way to recombine; that’s the whole interface. It’s embarrassingly parallel, robust, and works on problems where SGD literally can’t go.

This is the substrate for the deep dive at 3:50. When we talk about hierarchical evolutionary systems, this is the engine running at every layer.

Energy-based models

Train the landscape. Let physics handle generation.

  • 1982: Hopfield network. Associative memory as energy minimization. Patterns are attractor valleys; corrupted inputs roll downhill to the stored pattern.
  • 1985: Boltzmann machine (Hinton & Sejnowski). A stochastic EBM; restricted/deep variants became layered.
  • 2006: Hinton’s deep belief networks kick off the modern deep learning era. EBM-flavored.
  • 2019-20: Score-based generative modeling (Song & Ermon, Ho et al.) connects EBMs to diffusion.
  • 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics: Hopfield & Hinton.
  • The connection: diffusion is closely related to score / energy-based modeling. It learns local slope-like directions in the data landscape.
  • Metaphor: If you have an orchestra, an LLM would be like telling a single flautist to play the next note. An EBM would be the next step for the full orchestra.
  • Where it shows up: image / video / audio / 3D / protein backbones, associative memory, anomaly detection.

full story & further reading below ↓

The full story

↑ Energy-based models (top)

Energy-based models are old. Physicists thought about this problem before computer scientists did. The framing: define a scalar energy function E(x) over the space of possible configurations. Train it so that configurations the model thinks are “right” (real images, plausible sentences, valid protein structures) sit in low-energy valleys. Train it so everything else has high energy. To generate, you start from some random configuration and follow the gradient downhill until you land in one of the valleys.

1982 - Hopfield networks. The first really clean version. The network stores patterns as attractor states. Show it a noisy or partial version of one of those patterns, and the dynamics roll it downhill into the original. Associative memory, expressed as energy minimization. This is the Hopfield in the 2024 Nobel.

1985 - Boltzmann machines (Hinton & Sejnowski). Stochastic, more capable, but intractable in the general case. Restricted Boltzmann machines made a constrained version workable; deep variants stacked those pieces. 2006 - Hinton’s deep belief networks built on RBMs and helped kick off the modern deep learning era. The whole thing was EBM-flavored before backprop-on-feedforward-nets took over the mainstream.

The 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics went to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton “for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks.” This isn’t a soft analogy - the underlying math is statistical mechanics. Boltzmann distributions, partition functions, free energy. Hopfield networks are mathematically tied to spin-glass energy models.

The modern connection: diffusion models are closely related to score / energy-based modeling. They don’t learn an energy function directly - they learn local score-like directions. From a random sample, you follow that field toward a high-density region. That’s the intuition Stable Diffusion uses under the hood. Yang Song & Stefano Ermon’s 2019 score-based paper was one of the bridges that made this explicit.

Why this matters: it’s a fundamentally different theory of what it means for a model to “know” something. Not “predict the next token.” Not “minimize cross-entropy on a training set.” Instead: be at low energy near plausible data, high energy everywhere else. The math is physics, not linguistics. The intuitions transfer differently. The limits are different.

Neurosymbolic AI

Perception + logic. Intuition + proof. Glue the two AI traditions together.

  • 1956: Dartmouth Conference. Two AI camps from day one.
  • Symbolic / GOFAI: logic, rules, theorem provers. Composable, verifiable. Dominant through the 80s.
  • Connectionist / NN: perception, pattern matching, fuzzy reasoning. Took over after 2012 (AlexNet).
  • Each pure approach hits a ceiling. Symbolic = brittle (I would also argue unscalable). Neural = stochastic & hallucinates.
  • Garcez & Lamb (2020): call neurosymbolic the third wave.
  • Patterns: neural-then-symbolic pipeline · differentiable logic · program synthesis with neural priors · LLM + formal verifier.
  • Flagship: AlphaProof / AlphaGeometry (2024). Callback to the game-changers section.
  • Domain fit: anywhere you need both perception and verifiable correctness.
  • You could think of “Agentic” systems as a form of this honestly. What we now call the “agent harness” is the more symbolic part.

full story & further reading below ↓

The full story

↑ Neurosymbolic AI (top)

AI as a field was named at the 1956 Dartmouth Conference, and there have been two distinct camps from day one. They’ve been feuding ever since.

Symbolic AI (“Good Old-Fashioned AI”, or GOFAI): logic, rules, knowledge graphs, theorem provers, expert systems. Things you can write down. Things you can prove correct. Composable - if you know two rules, you can derive a third. Dominant through the 1970s and 80s. Got the first big AI funding, gave us LISP, gave us Cyc, gave us expert systems. Hit a wall in the late 80s when it couldn’t scale to perception and the messy real world. First AI winter.

Connectionist AI (neural networks): perception, pattern matching, fuzzy reasoning. Statistical, not logical. Powered by data and gradient descent. Mostly out of favor until 2012, when AlexNet won ImageNet and the modern deep learning era kicked off. Since then it’s dominated. LLMs are the current crescendo.

Neurosymbolic AI is the hybrid bet: glue them together. Use neural for perception and proposal, use symbolic for verification and composition. Garcez & Lamb (2020) called this “the third wave” - first symbolic, then connectionist, now hybrid.

The architectural patterns vary:

  • Neural-then-symbolic pipeline. A neural network perceives, hands a structured representation to a classical reasoner (planner, theorem prover, knowledge graph engine).
  • Differentiable logic. Embed logical operators into the neural network so the whole thing trains end-to-end with gradient descent. Logic Tensor Networks, DeepProbLog.
  • Program synthesis with neural priors. A neural model proposes programs; an interpreter checks if they actually work.
  • Language model + formal verifier. A language-model/search system proposes proof or code steps; a formal system (Lean, Coq, a type checker) accepts or rejects each one. This is the AlphaProof pattern and one of the cleanest patterns right now.

Research Deep Dive

3:50 to 4:30 · hierarchical evolutionary systems with orchestration

Why hierarchy, why evolution, why orchestration

  • Why evolution: Gradients (like in LLMs) need smooth differentiable objectives. Most interesting objectives aren’t.
  • Why hierarchy: Single-scale search plateaus. Stack search inside search and the plateau breaks.
  • Why orchestration: Evolution is stupid (read: directionless). Intelligence at an orchestration level allows a target.
  • Dynamic Fitness: Allowing fitness functions to change is the generalization ingredient.
  • Hierarchy Both Ways: You can break down the model into composables, why not the task into atomic parts?

Thesis: composable pieces in a stacked solution evo search, glued together by something that can reason about the stack itself.

full story & further reading below ↓

The full story

↑ Why hierarchy / evolution / orchestration (top)

Why evolution at the substrate. Gradient descent is great when you have a smooth, differentiable objective. The reality is that most interesting objectives aren’t smooth or differentiable. Composability (“did this combination of behaviors achieve the goal?”). Novelty (“is this idea actually new?”). Sparse reward (“the only signal is success or failure after 10,000 actions”). Gradient descent struggles in all of these. Evolution doesn’t care. It needs a scoring function and a way to recombine, and that’s it. Discrete, non-differentiable, black-box. Doesn’t matter.

Why hierarchy. A single evolutionary loop converges, then plateaus. It can’t redefine what fitness means on its own; it can only climb the fitness landscape it was handed. So stack a second loop above the first. The upper loop’s candidates are configurations of the lower loop. Now the system can climb both in fitness space and in fitness-definition space. The plateau breaks. Then do it again with a third loop on top of the second. Each higher rung is search over the search-spaces of the rung below.

Why orchestration at the top. Pure stacked search has a problem: it has no notion of “what are we trying to accomplish right now.” It’ll happily climb whatever fitness landscape it was started on, even if the world changed underneath it. The orchestrator is the part that reads the behavior of the stack and re-aims it. Picks which subproblem to prioritize. Reallocates compute. Decides when a layer has converged on something useless and needs a fresh objective.

The thesis: stacked search + something that reasons about the stack itself. Stacked search alone is just nested optimization. With an orchestrator on top, it becomes an AI system - it has a notion of goals, of what’s working, of when to pivot. When to even kick off the evolutionary part.

Why this might map onto consciousness research is the next section’s question. But the structural intuition is here: it looks a lot like the predictive processing picture of how a mind works.

The original loop

Before NEXUS, the idea was brute-force evolution over things that could solve.

  • Start with a population of candidate programs or models.
  • Mutate them, then ask three gates: does it compile, does it output, does the output pass a judge?
  • Early judge: dictionary words + Grammarly-style grammar checks.
  • Lesson: evolution can explore weird solution space, but without direction it wastes absurd compute.

So it became: let search invent solutions, but give it a steering system.

full story below ↓

The full story

↑ The original loop (top)

The starting point was not a polished research system. It was random mutation over code. Take a candidate, mutate it, compile it, run it, judge the output, keep what survives. At first the judge was extremely crude: did the C or C++ compile, did the program emit anything, and did the emitted text look less wrong under a grammar checker?

This was back in 2004-2005 high school days and into 2007’ish era. Was a bit of a shit programmer back then.

That crude setup still taught the important lesson. Evolution does not need a differentiable objective. It can search over code, networks, architectures, hyperparameters, and discrete choices. The problem is that unguided evolution is directionless. It can eventually find things, but only after wasting most of its budget on nonsense.

That is where the next ingredient enters: an orchestration layer that does not hand-design the answer, but does decide where search pressure should go next.

Evolution needs a driver

Evolution is the engine. The orchestrator is the steering wheel.

  • Pure evolution mutates and selects, but it does not understand the problem.
  • A heavy-handed orchestrator would just smuggle human assumptions back in.
  • The goal is light-touch: re-aim search, change pressure, allocate compute, decide when a path is stuck.
  • This leaves room for non-human kinds of intelligence to emerge.

Useful search needs pressure without becoming hand-coded intelligence.

Stack the search

full story below ↓

The full story

↑ Stack the search (top)

The algorithm grows in layers.

Layer 0 evolves candidate solvers for the problem. These are small neural networks with the only constraint that they are composable with each other.

Layer 1 evolves the conditions for Layer 0: population size, mutation operators, architecture search space, fitness probes, selection pressure, and when to stop.

Layer 2 changes the task pressure itself. If the current objective is too blunt, it can change the fitness function, introduce novelty pressure, or split the problem into smaller atomic parts.

The orchestrator watches all of this. It does not need to know the exact solution. It needs to know when search is converging, when it is wandering, when it is overfitting to one pressure, and when the problem needs to be decomposed.

Dynamic fitness

The functions are allowed to morph.

  • Static fitness finds specialists.
  • Changing fitness can pressure robustness, transfer, and broader behavior.
  • The orchestrator can adjust the target when the system plateaus or the world changes.
  • Important caveat: dynamic fitness may help generalization, but it does not guarantee it.

Break the problem apart

Hard problems are often bundles of smaller priors.

  • Check whether a known solver already exists.
  • If not, evolve a solver for the whole problem.
  • If that fails, split the task into atomic priors or subskills.
  • Evolve small models for those pieces, then assemble them into a chain or graph.

Instead of one giant model learning everything at once, search for reusable pieces.

Mini-models become a solver graph

The successful chain becomes a reusable solution, not just a one-off answer.

full story below ↓

The full story

↑ Mini-models become a solver graph (top)

The ARC-AGI mindset is useful here. A puzzle may require objectness, counting, symmetry, gravity, color remapping, or containment. Those are not full solutions by themselves. They are priors or tiny abilities the full solution depends on.

The proposed solver algorithm is:

  1. Try known solvers first.
  2. If none solve it, evolve a candidate solver.
  3. If the whole-problem solver fails after a threshold, split the problem.
  4. Evolve small models for the pieces.
  5. Chain those mini-models into a graph.
  6. Validate the whole graph against the original problem.
  7. If it works, store it as a reusable program-like solver in a DSL search space.

This starts to look like program synthesis, but with evolved neural pieces as the reusable operators. It also starts to look like a graph neural network at the system level: each node is a solver, each edge is a dependency or data handoff, and the graph itself becomes the candidate.

The blocker: representation

The solver graph needs a shared language for the world.

  • The evolved solvers need to agree on what they are seeing.
  • Existing models usually bake the encoder into the task model.
  • That helps end-to-end training, but couples representation to one objective.
  • The missing piece was a task-free world model: a shared substrate downstream solvers can speak.

That is where NEXUS (Neural Evolution for eXtensible Universal Semantics) broke off into its own research thread.

NEXUS split off

NEXUS is world code. Downstream models are task speakers of that code.

  • NEXUS: evolve a universal conceptual substrate from multimodal experience.
  • Downstream solvers: use that substrate for tasks, actions, language, planning, or ARC-style problem solving.
  • It is not a next-token model and not a classifier.
  • It is a shared perceptual filter and concept hub.

Step 1: build the substrate before asking it to reason about itself.

full story below ↓

The full story

↑ NEXUS split off (top)

The transcript question was exactly the core blocker: what is the perception layer? How does the system know what problem it is looking at?

NEXUS is the answer to that blocker. Instead of training a task model and letting its encoder get shaped by that task, NEXUS separates the encoder into its own upstream system. Its job is not to solve the task. Its job is to place experience into a persistent latent space where related things line up across modality and time.

That gives downstream models a common substrate. A model that talks, a model that plans, a model that solves visual puzzles, and a model that controls tools can all receive the same kind of world code instead of each learning an isolated representation.

The tradeoff is real: if the encoder is separate, task loss does not automatically improve it. So NEXUS has to evolve representation quality directly. Each version is frozen and updates invalidate downstream models (a touch of fine-tuning should correct (TBD))

What NEXUS ingests

Experience is a temporal tree, not a bag of examples.

  • Dataset: https://huggingface.co/datasets/Ardea/NEXUS-temporal_hierarchical_multi-modal.
  • Base unit: 10ms slices with synchronized audio and visual references.
  • Higher levels: moments, seconds, experiences, minutes, then extensible levels above that.
  • Current data includes audio, frames, phonemes, words, gestures, actions, and modality slots for future sensors.

The point is continuous multimodal experience, not image-caption pairs.

What NEXUS evolves

The evolved object is the encoder and substrate, not a task answer.

full story below ↓

The full story

↑ What NEXUS evolves (top)

The current implementation samples genomes that describe encoder architectures: layer types, layer count, latent dimension, pooling, context window, and related hyperparameters. It builds a model from that genome, streams temporal slices through it, stores embeddings in NexusState, and evolves the population over recent windows of experience.

The fitness is intentionally task-free. The current scaffold scores representation quality through probes:

  • Diversity / anti-collapse: different inputs should not all become the same vector.
  • Stability: small audio or image perturbations should not destroy the representation.
  • Cross-modal alignment: audio-only, visual-only, and full embeddings should agree when they describe the same moment.
  • Rank / entropy: the embedding space should use capacity without collapsing.
  • Complexity controls: optional pressure against needlessly large encoders.

The supported strategy set is broad enough to test search behavior: GA, speciation, islands, MAP-Elites, NSGA-II, NSGA-III, novelty search, AFPO, and ALPS.

Concepts as hyperedges

A concept is not one token. It is a path through time and modality.

  • “Apple” should connect written word, spoken word, image, bite sound, action, context.
  • NEXUS tracks concepts across the temporal hierarchy.
  • Relations can be temporal, causal, compositional, similarity, association, or hierarchy.
  • The concept layer is the bridge from raw experience to reusable world structure.

The substrate is trying to make the same thing point to the same place.

Built and running

  • Streaming ingestion, temporal hierarchy, genome sampling, model building, state updates, and evolution loop are wired.
  • 21 ablations completed: temporal levels, fixed architectures, fitness components, concept / relation toggles, and strategy variants.
  • Multiple strategies have produced complete runs: GA, speciation, islands, MAP-Elites, NSGA-II, NSGA-III, novelty, ALPS.

Early signals

  • Concept report: 10 / 10 concepts were multi-level hyperedges.
  • Cross-modal report: 10 / 10 tracked concepts had audio + visual membership; 4 / 10 also included text.

Early substrate signals are the right kind of weird. Seems to be working.

Where it plugs back in

NEXUS is the perception substrate for the solver algorithm.

  • The original solver graph needs priors and shared representation.
  • NEXUS supplies a stable world code for those priors.
  • The solver side can evolve task models on top.
  • Successful solver graphs can feed back as reusable structures, not just outputs.

NEXUS is the part that lets the evolutionary system know what its pieces mean.

Why this points at consciousness research

Trying to get to a good foundation first.

  • Step 1: NEXUS builds a persistent conceptual substrate.
  • Step 2: downstream reasoning / planning systems speak that substrate.
  • Step 3: a self-model and metacognitive layer can inspect and redirect the whole system.
  • The big question: will a persistent substrate + evolved solvers + self-observation give us machine consciousness? If so, now I have to figure out how to test that.

This is a research program, not a claim that the system is conscious.

full story & further reading below ↓

The full story

↑ Consciousness research (top)

The cautious framing matters. Consciousness is easy to over-claim and hard to define. NEXUS does not prove consciousness, and the current implementation is not an agent with inner experience.

The reason it belongs near consciousness research is structural. A persistent world substrate gives the system something like stable perception. Evolved solver graphs give it non-human routes to problem solving. An orchestrator gives it a way to redirect effort. A future self-model would give it something to inspect: not just the outside world, but its own substrate, solver graph, goals, and failures.

That maps onto several serious research frames without collapsing into any one of them:

  • Predictive processing: hierarchical models update themselves against prediction error.
  • Active inference: perception and action are framed as one loop of minimizing surprise.
  • Global workspace: selected information becomes globally available to many specialist systems.
  • Open-ended evolution: the system keeps generating new goals, niches, and pressures instead of converging once.

NEXUS is Step 1 of (what I think will be) a three-part research goal. Build the substrate. Build systems that reason through it. Then study whether self-modeling over that substrate creates measurable properties relevant to consciousness.
